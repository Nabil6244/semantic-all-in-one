Rome was founded by a boy raised by a wolf who murdered his own twin brother over a wall. Also, that story is almost certainly fake. What’s real is somehow weirder. A muddy village on seven hills, with no great army and no great leader, grew into an empire that ruled somewhere around a fifth of humanity, and then spent roughly a thousand years refusing to die. So grab a snack, because we’re covering about 1,200 years of history, and yes, it’s a mess.

Let’s start with the legend, because the Romans loved it. The story says twin brothers, Romulus and Remus, were abandoned as babies, rescued by a she-wolf, and grew up to found a city in 753 BC. They argued about where to build it, Romulus killed Remus, and he named the city after himself. Healthy sibling relationship. Historians doubt all of this. What archaeology shows is a cluster of villages along the Tiber River, in central Italy, slowly merging into one town around the eighth century BC. The location was clever, though. It sat on a river crossing, near the sea but not too near, with hills to defend. Real estate really is everything.

Early Rome was ruled by kings, and not always Roman ones. To the north lived the Etruscans, a sophisticated civilization that was richer and fancier than the Latin villagers. Etruscan kings ended up ruling Rome and, to be fair, they upgraded it. Drainage, public buildings, temples. Rome went from a village to an actual city. But here’s the problem with kings: they stay kings until they become awful. Tradition says the last one, Tarquin the Proud, was so tyrannical and so hated that in 509 BC the Roman nobles threw him out. The story attaches this to a scandal involving his son, but the real point is that Romans decided they never wanted a king again. And they meant it. For centuries, the word “king” was basically an insult in Rome.

So what replaced the king? A republic, and this is where Rome gets clever. Think of it as a company where nobody is allowed to be CEO alone. Instead of one king, they elected two leaders called consuls, and each could veto the other. Their term lasted exactly one year, so nobody got comfortable. Above them sat the Senate, a council of powerful, rich men who advised, debated, and quietly ran everything. It was designed to stop any single person from grabbing too much power. Remember that design, because it’s going to be tested to destruction later.

Now, inside this republic there was a fight brewing. Rome was split between two classes. The patricians were the old noble families, who held all the important jobs. The plebeians were everyone else, the farmers, the craftsmen, and the soldiers. And here’s the thing: the plebeians were the ones fighting in the army. So the plebeians basically said, “Fine, we’ll stop fighting for you unless you give us a say.” They literally went on strike. Over time, they won their own officials called tribunes, who could block unfair laws, and around 450 BC, Rome wrote down its laws on the Twelve Tables so that nobody could secretly twist them. This long argument is called the Conflict of the Orders, and it’s a big reason Rome stayed stable. Fighting over power inside the system, instead of tearing the system apart, turned out to be a superpower.

And Rome needed that stability, because the neighbors were not friendly. Around 390 BC, a Gallic tribe led by a chieftain named Brennus beat the Roman army and sacked the city. Rome was burned and humiliated. Most of it, anyway. Tradition says the Gauls left after being paid off in gold. You’d think that would finish Rome. Instead, the Romans rebuilt, learned from it, and started doing something nobody else did quite like them. They conquered neighbors and then, instead of just crushing them, offered them a deal. Be our allies, send us soldiers, and some of you can even become Roman citizens. Think of it like a franchise model. Every conquered town added manpower, and Rome never ran out of soldiers. Over the next two centuries, through a grinding series of wars against groups like the Samnites and Latin cities, Rome took over most of the Italian peninsula. Even a Greek king named Pyrrhus beat them in battle, but lost so many men doing it that “Pyrrhic victory” became a phrase. Winning so hard you lose is still losing.

By about 270 BC, Rome ruled Italy. And across the sea, there was a problem. Carthage. A wealthy city in modern Tunisia, founded by Phoenician traders, which controlled the western Mediterranean with a powerful navy. Two superpowers, one sea. Oh, I wonder if that could cause a problem.

The First Punic War started in 264 BC, over Sicily. Rome had no real navy, which is a bit of a handicap when fighting the greatest sea power around. So the Romans did what Romans do. They copied a captured Carthaginian ship, built hundreds of their own, and added a boarding bridge nicknamed the corvus, which let them turn sea battles into land fights, where they were dominant. After more than twenty years of war, Rome won in 241 BC and took Sicily as its first overseas province. Carthage was furious, and not in a “let’s move on” way.

Enter Hannibal. In 218 BC, this Carthaginian general started the Second Punic War, and he did something that sounds like a video game cheat code. Instead of fighting Rome by sea, he marched an army, with war elephants, from Spain over the Alps into Italy. In winter. Many men and elephants died on the way, but he arrived and started winning. Then came 216 BC, the Battle of Cannae. The Romans had a much larger army, and Hannibal surrounded and destroyed it in a single afternoon. Tens of thousands of Romans were killed, one of the worst days in Roman history. Rome was shaking.

Here’s what Hannibal expected: that Rome’s allies would abandon it. Some did. But most stayed loyal. That franchise model from earlier? It paid off. Rome simply kept raising new armies, avoided fighting Hannibal head-on, and let him wander around Italy for years without being able to finish the job. Then a young Roman general named Scipio had a clever idea. If Hannibal won’t leave Italy, attack his home. Scipio invaded Africa, forcing Hannibal to rush back, and in 202 BC, at the Battle of Zama, Scipio won. He earned the title “Africanus” for it, which is a pretty good nickname to get.

Carthage survived for a while, but Rome never trusted it. In the 140s BC, the Romans picked a fight again, and in 146 BC, the Third Punic War ended with Carthage destroyed. You may have heard that Romans salted the earth there. That’s very likely a later legend, so we’ll file it under entertaining but unproven. In that same year, Rome also crushed the Greek city of Corinth. Within about a century, Rome had gone from one Italian city to the ruler of the Mediterranean. And the Romans, being practical, called the Mediterranean “our sea.”

Now, you’d think winning everything would make everyone happy. It did not. All that conquest brought in wealth, slaves, and land, and almost all of it flowed to the rich. Small Roman farmers, the very men who’d fought the wars, came home to find their farms failing or taken over by huge estates worked by enslaved people. Rome was richer than ever, and its citizens were poorer than ever. Sound familiar?

Around 133 BC, a tribune named Tiberius Gracchus tried to fix it by redistributing public land to poor citizens. A reasonable idea, but the Senate hated it, and a mob of senators beat him to death. Yes, in the actual Senate area, with actual sticks. His younger brother Gaius tried again a decade later, and he also ended up dead. And that’s the turning point. For the first time, Roman politics turned violent in a serious way. The rules of the game were cracking.

Next came the strongmen. A general named Marius reformed the army, letting poor men join and get paid. Great for recruitment, but here’s the catch: those soldiers now felt loyal to their general, who paid them and promised them land, not to Rome. Armies were becoming personal. Marius had a rival, Sulla, and in 88 BC Sulla did something nobody had ever done. He marched his army into Rome itself. Later he made himself dictator, executed his enemies on public lists, and then, surprisingly, retired. But the lesson was learned. If you have a loyal army, you can take Rome. And a lot of ambitious men were paying attention.

There was also a famous slave revolt in this era, led by Spartacus, a gladiator. In 73 BC he and a few dozen others escaped, and the movement grew to tens of thousands of followers. They defeated several Roman armies before being crushed in 71 BC. It’s one of the best-known stories from Rome, though it’s worth remembering that most of the details come from Roman writers, not from Spartacus himself.

Now we reach three giants. Pompey, a brilliant and popular general. Crassus, the richest man in Rome. And Julius Caesar, ambitious, charming, and drowning in debt. In 60 BC, they teamed up in an unofficial alliance called the First Triumvirate. Think of it as three CEOs who don’t trust each other agreeing to split the company. These deals never last.

Caesar went off to conquer Gaul, roughly modern France, between 58 and 50 BC. He won, became incredibly famous, and made his soldiers fiercely loyal to him. Meanwhile, Crassus died in a disastrous campaign against Parthia, and the alliance collapsed. The Senate sided with Pompey and ordered Caesar to give up his army. Caesar thought about it. Then, in January 49 BC, he led his troops across the Rubicon River, the border of Italy, which was against the law. That’s where we get the phrase “crossing the Rubicon,” meaning no turning back. Civil war began. Caesar beat Pompey, chased him to Egypt, got involved with Cleopatra, and returned as the most powerful man in Rome.

In 44 BC he was named dictator for life. The Senate’s reaction was basically, “Wait, isn’t this the exact thing we promised never to allow?” On the Ides of March, March 15, a group of senators led by Brutus and Cassius stabbed him to death. They thought they were saving the Republic. Instead, they triggered another round of civil war. Plot twist: the Republic was already dead. They just killed the guy it died for.

Caesar’s will named his teenage great-nephew, Octavian, as heir. Nobody took the kid seriously. Big mistake. Octavian teamed up with Mark Antony and Lepidus in the Second Triumvirate, hunted down Caesar’s killers, and then, once those enemies were gone, the allies turned on each other. Lepidus was pushed aside. Antony ran off to Egypt and joined Cleopatra. In 31 BC, at the naval Battle of Actium, Octavian’s forces won. Antony and Cleopatra both died the following year, and Egypt became part of Rome.

Now Octavian was the only man standing, and he was smart enough to know what killed Caesar. Don’t look like a king. In 27 BC he pretended to hand power back to the Senate, and the grateful Senate gave him a new title, Augustus. He kept control of the army, the money, and the key offices while keeping the old republic’s costumes on. It was a monarchy wearing a republic’s clothes. He’s the first Roman emperor, even though he never used that word. And it worked. Augustus ruled for more than forty years, and his reign started the Pax Romana, roughly two centuries of relative peace and prosperity across the empire.

Of course, peace didn’t mean good emperors. His successors were a mixed bag. Tiberius was gloomy and suspicious. Caligula was young, spent wildly, and was assassinated after about four years. Claudius, who people had underestimated, turned out to be a capable ruler. Then came Nero, who is said to have played music while Rome burned in 64 AD. That’s probably an exaggeration, since he may not even have been in the city, but he did blame Christians for the fire, which began a wave of persecution. Nero lost the support of the army and died in 68. And just when you thought things couldn’t get worse, 69 AD became the Year of Four Emperors. Four men claimed the throne in twelve months. Last one standing was Vespasian, who started a new dynasty and built the Colosseum.

After that, Rome entered its golden age. Emperors like Trajan, who in 117 AD ruled the empire at its largest size, from Britain to Mesopotamia. Hadrian, who built a wall across northern Britain and spent much of his reign traveling around his empire. Then Marcus Aurelius, the philosopher emperor, who wrote down his private thoughts on being a good person while fighting wars on the frontier. Roads, aqueducts, law, trade. A person could travel across the Mediterranean world and find similar cities, similar coins, and similar laws. Not bad for a village that started with a wolf story.

But under the surface, cracks were growing. A terrible plague, the Antonine Plague, swept through the empire in the 160s and hurt both population and army. Marcus Aurelius died in 180, and instead of picking a capable successor, he left the throne to his son Commodus, who was more interested in playing gladiator than ruling. He was murdered in 192, and the empire again slid into civil war.

Then came the Crisis of the Third Century, roughly 235 to 284 AD. Imagine a company where the CEO changes almost every two years, and most of them are fired violently. Over about fifty years, there were dozens of claimants to the throne, many killed by their own soldiers. Barbarian tribes pushed across borders. Prices spiked because emperors kept making coins with less silver in them, which is a classic way to wreck an economy. The empire even split into pieces for a time. And somehow, it survived.

It survived thanks to a tough soldier named Diocletian, who took power in 284. He realized one man couldn’t run something this huge, so he divided the work among four rulers, called the Tetrarchy. He also reformed taxes and the army. It stabilized things, but it didn’t last. After he retired, the rulers fought each other, and a man named Constantine came out on top. In 312, before the Battle of the Milvian Bridge, he reportedly had a vision connected to the Christian faith, won, and then in 313 helped issue the Edict of Milan, which allowed Christians to worship freely. Within decades, Christianity would go from persecuted to the favored religion of the empire. In 330, Constantine also founded a new capital in the east, Constantinople, on the site of the old Greek city of Byzantium. Remember that city. It’s going to matter a lot.

By the late 300s, the empire’s biggest problem wasn’t just internal. Groups beyond the frontier were being pushed by other peoples, notably the Huns, and many were now entering Roman land. In 378, at the Battle of Adrianople, Goths crushed a Roman army and killed Emperor Valens. In 395, after the death of Theodosius, the empire was permanently divided between east and west, ruled from separate capitals. The east, centered on Constantinople, was richer and easier to defend. The west was poorer, weaker, and surrounded.

Then, in 410, a Gothic leader named Alaric did what no foreign army had done in about eight hundred years. He entered Rome and sacked it. It was a huge psychological shock. The city wasn’t the capital anymore, but it was still Rome. In 455, the Vandals sacked it again, and the western emperors became puppets, controlled by generals of Germanic background. Think of it as a company where the board members are fighting and the real power sits with the security guards.

Finally, in 476, the Germanic commander Odoacer removed the last western emperor, a teenager with a deliciously ironic name, Romulus Augustulus. That is, “little Augustus,” named after the city’s first king and its first emperor. Odoacer didn’t even bother to appoint a replacement. He just sent the imperial regalia to Constantinople. That’s the date most people use for the fall of Rome. Though honestly, it wasn’t a sudden collapse. Historians often describe it as a long, messy transformation, and for people living through it, many probably didn’t wake up and think, “Today, Rome ended.”

And here’s the twist. The Roman Empire didn’t actually end in 476. The eastern half carried on for almost another thousand years. We usually call it the Byzantine Empire, but its people called themselves Romans the whole time. Under Justinian in the 500s, it even reconquered parts of the west and organized Roman law into a code that influenced legal systems for centuries. It endured plagues, wars, and invasions, until 1453, when the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II captured Constantinople. Only then did the last remnant of Rome fall, about 2,200 years after the first legend of Romulus.

So what’s the answer to the big question? How did a village become an empire, and why did it fall? The rise came from a strange mix. Flexible politics, a talent for turning enemies into allies, ruthless persistence, and an army that kept coming back after disasters like Cannae. The decline came from the opposite. Ambition without limits, armies loyal to generals, civil wars that drained everything, and an empire too big and too expensive to protect. Rome’s great strength, its willingness to adapt, only worked when its leaders cared more about Rome than themselves.

And maybe that’s why this story still feels so familiar. Rome’s laws, languages, roads, and ideas about government are still around us. It reminds us that even the mightiest systems depend on people willing to follow the rules, and that they rarely collapse in a single day. They wear down slowly, until nobody notices that the last pillar has already fallen.
