If you ever wake up at three in the morning, and see a deer standing in your yard, do not wave. Do not call out to it. And whatever you do, do not turn on a light. I am not talking about a normal deer. A normal deer bolts the moment a porch door opens. This one has stood in the same patch of grass for three nights. It is far too tall, with legs that bend in all the wrong places, and it never once lowers its head to eat. The first night I told myself it was lost. The second night I stopped telling myself anything. Every house on my street was dark, and every window faced the same way. The strangest part is where it looks. It is not looking at your house. It is looking at something just behind you. My grandmother called them the Watchers, and she kept a list. I asked around, and nobody else had seen it, or nobody else would say so. That is when I found her list, folded inside an old bible. Seven lines in pencil, and the third one was underlined twice. Follow these seven rules and you will see the sun come up. Most people break the third one, because it sounds like someone you love. Do not turn on the lights. Light tells it exactly where you are. Even a phone screen counts, so turn it face down and keep it there. A man named Hale, two streets over, flicked on his porch bulb. The deer turned its head all the way around, Hale says it was only a second, and the second is still going. Now his whole street leaves its curtains open, in case it looks back. and Hale's porch has been dark ever since. Do not count its legs. Everyone wants to. The legs are wrong, and you want to prove it. A woman at my old job counted them out loud. The number keeps changing, which is how it keeps you counting. She got to nine, and then she stopped getting to anything. If you have to look, look at its head and nowhere lower. Do not answer the door. Around four it will knock, three soft taps. Then it speaks, and it uses a voice you know. Three taps, a pause, and then a fourth that does not belong to the rhythm. Your mother. Your brother. The friend who moved away. It will say it is cold. It will say please. My cousin opened it and said it was only for a second. We still set a place for him, and nobody sits there. It has never once got the pause right between the words. If it moves, stay exactly where you are. The first night it stands at the fence. The second night it stands at the porch. The third night it is simply closer, and you never see it move. Do not measure the distance. Distance is the whole game. Count the seconds instead, and count them slowly. If you run, it learns that you can be made to run. Stillness is the only thing it has never learned to copy. It can learn your voice and your walk, but not how long you can wait. Do not speak to anyone who says they are outside. Your phone will light up with a neighbour's name. They will say they can see it too and ask you to come out. Their messages are perfect, with every comma in its place. My sister replied once, from a bed three towns away. She said she was fine, and I believed her, and that was the mistake. Your neighbours are asleep. They are always asleep. If you reply, the typing dots start, and they never stop. Do not follow the sound of your name. It calls from the hallway, then from the stairs, then from the walls. The voice is warm and a little out of breath. It sounds like someone running up the stairs to tell you good news. Give it nothing. Not a word, not a breath, not a turn of the head. A voice that is looking for you has never needed to ask where you are. Stay in bed, and let it say your name until it forgets how. Do not sleep until the sun is fully up. Sunrise is a promise, and it keeps it. The yard will empty a little before the light arrives. Not all at once. It leaves the way a held breath leaves. Wait for the birds. Wait for the first car. Wait. My grandmother waited eleven years of mornings like that. She was never once found in the yard. There is one thing I have not told you yet. After the third night I stopped being afraid of the deer. Because I finally worked out what it was looking at. Something had been living in my house longer than I had. It moved when I slept. It stood where I stood. Every night it practised how to look like me, It got the way I tilt my head, and the way I hold a door. It learned the sound I make when I clear my throat. and every night the deer stood in the yard, watching it, close enough to be seen and far enough not to be reached. The deer was never the danger. The deer was the warning. Think about it. A predator would hide. The deer stood in the open, under the moon, so that I would look. I watched my own bedroom light flicker on, and I was in bed. I was lying there, and I was also the one standing at the switch. That is when the deer turned and looked at me for the first time. It did not look angry. It looked sorry. Then I understood the seventh rule, and why my grandmother never slept. It is not the deer you have to outlast. It is the other one. The one that wears your sleep like a coat and waits for you to forget. I scratched the list into the bedpost, one rule for every night. Not to keep it out, because it was never outside. Only to remind the one in the bed which of us is real. And behind me, very softly, someone said my name. So if you ever wake up at three in the morning and see a deer standing in your yard, stay in bed, keep the lights off, and be very kind to the deer. It is the only one who has been watching out for you.
