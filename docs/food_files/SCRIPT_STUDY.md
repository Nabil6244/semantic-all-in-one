# Food Files — competitor script study (2026-11-22)

All 8 @stevetalksfood scripts, cleaned from their captions and measured, plus a close read of the top two
(Candy 5.6 M, Snacks 2.3 M) against the weakest two (Chains 136 K, Thanksgiving 100 K). This is the spec our scripts
follow. Patterns only: we write our own stories, words and jokes.

| Thanksgiving | 100 K | 15.5 | 177 | 8 | ~340 | 5 | 16 |

- **Length:** 2,500–3,250 words, 14–19 min, ~172 words a minute (fast but clear).
- **Items:** 8–13, ~90 s each (250–400 words). More items, not longer items.
- **Sentences:** average 14–15 words. **~40 % start with "And / But / So"** — every sentence leans on the one before.
- **Numbers:** 4–6 per minute (dates, ages, counts, money, comparisons).
- **What separates the hits from the misses:** the topic and the people, not the craft (the craft is the same in all
  8). Hits: products everyone ate as a kid + real victims with a name, age, town and date. Misses: business stories
  with no victims (Chains: 0 people, 58 dates) and a narrow seasonal topic (Thanksgiving).

## 2. The cold open (first 15–25 s)

Same skeleton in all 8:

1. **"From [item A that did a shocking thing] to [item B that did another], these are the [deadliest X ever sold]."**
   The two teasers are from later in the video, never item 1, and each is a 7–12 word picture: a product + a
   physical consequence ("…that got toddlers high", "…that burned a hole through a boy's tongue", "…that sent a CEO
   to prison").
2. **"But first, we're starting with [item 1] because/cuz [why it's interesting]."** — straight into item 1, no
   intro, no channel talk, no "welcome back".
3. Item 1 is the most **recent / viral / recognisable** story of the set (One Chip Challenge, Panera charged
   lemonade, Wonka Gobstopper, Four Loko).

## 3. Anatomy of one item (~90 s)

| Beat | Share | What it does | Typical lines |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Name it | 5 % | "This is / We're talking about [brand product]" + what it is in one breath | "This is Pixy Stix… paper straws full of flavored sugar you pour straight into your mouth" |
| 2. Make it familiar | 10 % | memory, scale, a quirky detail | "you've probably eaten their products", "coffin-shaped box with a red skull" |
| 3. Set the trap | 10 % | why it seemed fine / the catch | "And there's a reason for that." "It looks completely harmless, right? Well…" |
| 4. The case | 25 % | date + place + named person + age, told as a mini story, then the outcome | "on September 1st, 2023… 14-year-old… a sophomore from Worcester, Massachusetts…" |
| 5. The why | 15 % | the science / mechanism in 2–3 plain steps, often with a comparison | "a jalapeno tops out at around 8,000 Scoville… the Reaper is over 2 million" |
| 6. The twist | 10 % | "But here's the part / the twist / the weird part / somehow it gets worse" — a second fact that raises the stakes | "…the owner sent an email that said 'Just ship it.'" |
| 7. The fallout | 15 % | recall, lawsuit, ban, sentence, numbers sick/dead, "in the end…", "as a result…" | "714 sick, 9 dead… 28 years in prison" |
| 8. The button | 5 % | one dry, short line of host opinion or irony | "See you later, Stewart." "Pretty ironic." "They really are slippery little snacks." |
| 9. The bridge | 5 % | links this item to the next by contrast (below) | |

Every item has a real case (named or specific people), at least two hard numbers, a fallout and a button.

## 4. Bridges (the glue between items)

Six templates cover almost every transition; the product is named in the very next sentence:

| Template | Shape |
|---|---|
| Contrast | "But while [A did X], this next [one did Y]." |
| At least | "But at least [A had a saving grace], because this next [one was worse]." |
| If only | "If only this next [one] came with [what was missing]…" |
| Unlike | "Unlike this next [one], which/cuz [hook]." |
| Kind of like | "Kind of like this next [one]…" (a shared trait) |
| Speaking of | "Speaking of [word from the last item], this next…" |

The bridge always states what kind of danger is coming ("…starts hurting the second it touches your tongue",
"…which blends right in with the others"), so the viewer has a reason to stay.

## 5. Retention devices

- **Re-hook in the middle** (~45–55 % in): "If you think [last item] is as bad as it gets, we still got [two teasers
  from the last third]."
- **Ordering:** most recognisable first; then alternate the *kind* of danger (physical injury → crime → mystery →
  legal oddity → choking → poison → gross-out comedy → corporate cover-up); keep 2–3 of the strongest stories for
  the last third; mix eras (1850s to this year).
- **Questions to the viewer:** 3–13 per video ("…still edible, right?", "Sounds fake, right?", "So what happened?").
- **Twist markers:** "here's the twist / the part / the thing / the creepy part / the weird part", "it gets worse",
  "believe it or not" — about one every two items.
- **Comparisons for scale:** "to put that in perspective", "for reference", "that's barely a handful".

## 6. The outro (last ~20 s)

1. One line of advice tied to the topic: "So the next time you [pour a bowl / hit the drive-thru], [check …]."
2. A link into a related video **as if it were the next item**: "And the next time you [related habit], be careful
   there too, because these are the deadliest [other topic] ever sold."
3. One teaser from that video: "…and one so dangerous it's been banned for 20 years."
4. "So click this video right here, and I'll catch you in the next one."

No recap, no "thanks for watching", no long subscribe ask (one video only: "like and subscribe").

## 7. Voice

- First person, casual, quick. Contractions and "cuz". Little jokes, never at a victim's expense.
- Short opinions as asides: "And you can see where this is going." "They don't mess around over there."
- "Allegedly" and "according to reports" for anything a court didn't settle; exact figures with their source.
- No gore described, no graphic detail of deaths: the fact, the age, the consequence.

## 8. Story selection (what makes a strong item)

Score each candidate (1 point each; keep 5+):
1. A product people know (a brand, or a type everyone has eaten).
2. A specific case: date, place, a person with an age.
3. A physical, easy-to-picture consequence.
4. A clear mechanism we can explain in 2–3 steps.
5. A fallout: recall, lawsuit, ban, prison, law.
6. A twist (cover-up, irony, "it happened again").
7. A visual we can actually show (Commons photo, recall notice, news clip).
8. Kids involved, or it's something kids eat.

Topic choice for a whole video: broad categories people grew up with beat business or seasonal angles; seasonal works
only as a once-a-year spike; part 2s of the winners (more candy, more snacks) are the safest next videos.

## 9. What our pipeline must enforce (→ script prompt + script checker)

- 2,500–3,100 words; 8–12 items; each item 220–420 words.
- Cold open in the skeleton above; item 1 is not one of the two teasers.
- Every item: a year, a place, a person or a specific group with a number, 2+ numbers, a fallout word, a button line.
- Bridges from the six templates; product named in the next sentence.
- A mid-video re-hook between 40 % and 60 %.
- Outro: advice + link to another of our videos + teaser + "click this video right here".
- ~40 % of sentences starting with And / But / So; average sentence 12–17 words.
