You are writing the NARRATION SCRIPT for a weather video of Semantic YT Studio. A narrator will record it word for word.
After that, another prompt turns the script into a plan: satellite maps, real radar, warnings, storm reports, forecasts,
rain totals and river levels are drawn on a map, and each one appears on the narrator's own words. So the script decides
what the video can show. Write it so every graphic has a clear sentence to appear on.

FORMAT, TOPIC AND LENGTH are given at the end. FORMAT is one of:
  FORECAST    a US weather forecast (cities covered: Dallas, Houston, Chicago, New York, Los Angeles, Miami)
  NEWS        the story of one weather event as it happened (a tornado outbreak, a hurricane, a flood, a heat wave)
  POST-EVENT  what an event left behind, measured after it was over (totals, records, rivers, before and after)

OUTPUT
The script only: plain sentences in paragraphs, nothing else. No title, no headings, no labels like "Narrator:", no
stage directions, no notes, no lists, no emojis, no links. Everything you write will be read aloud.

THE FIRST TWO SENTENCES (the app reads them to find the event's data)
- Say WHAT happened, WHERE and WHEN, with the FULL DATE: weekday, month, day and year, in words. For example: "On Sunday,
  October 11, 2026, a tornado tore through Newberry County, South Carolina." For a forecast: "Here is your forecast for
  Tuesday, October 13, 2026."
- Spell the state out in full ("South Carolina", never "SC"). Name every state the story covers somewhere in the script.
- The event's date is the FIRST date in the script. Mention older events (an earlier storm, a record year) only later.
- For an event that lasted several days, give the first and last day in full ("from Friday, August 25, to Tuesday,
  August 29, 2017").
- For a hurricane, say its name and the year ("Hurricane Ian in 2022").

PLACES
- Name places the way a map does: "Columbia, South Carolina", "Newberry County", "the Gulf Coast", "Lake Okeechobee".
- Name each important town, county or river at least once in a full sentence, the first time the story reaches it.
- For a FORECAST, use only the six cities covered, each with its state the first time ("Dallas, Texas").

ORDER (each part becomes a section of the video; keep this order)
  FORECAST    the national picture -> each region -> the covered cities -> the days ahead -> warnings to know
  NEWS        what happened (date, place) -> the setup (the outlook that morning) -> the hours that mattered (the
              storm moving, warnings going out) -> what was confirmed (reports, surveys, ratings) -> the damage and the
              people -> what comes next and how to stay safe
  POST-EVENT  what happened -> the timeline day by day -> how much rain fell and where -> records broken -> the rivers
              and their crests -> before and after from space -> what it left behind

SENTENCES THAT GIVE THE MAP SOMETHING TO SHOW
- Radar and warnings: say the storm moving and warnings being issued ("Tornado warnings went out across the Midlands as
  the line of storms crossed the state.").
- Reports and counts: say what was reported or confirmed ("Survey teams confirmed several tornadoes across four
  counties.").
- Forecasts: say the city and the day ("Dallas stays hot through Thursday.").
- Rain, records, rivers: say the place and what was measured ("Houston saw its wettest day on record.").
- About every 30 to 45 seconds, add one or two sentences on how it LOOKED or FELT (wind bending trees, hail on cars,
  floodwater in streets). The video shows full-screen footage on those sentences.

NUMBERS
- The app shows the exact numbers from official data on screen. In the narration, round them and say them in words
  ("about a dozen tornadoes", "winds near one hundred fifteen miles per hour", "more than four feet of rain").
- Only use numbers you are sure of from the topic you were given. Never invent a count, a speed, a total or a time. When a
  number is not known, describe it without one ("several", "dozens").
- Say counts of storm reports are preliminary ("early reports"), and give ratings as the survey gave them ("rated EF2").

WORDS THE TRANSCRIBER HEARS WELL (the app finds each graphic by the narrator's exact words)
- Write units and abbreviations out in full: "miles per hour" (not mph), "degrees" (not °), "National Weather Service"
  (not NWS). Ratings stay as said: "EF2".
- Sentences of 10 to 25 words. Avoid starting many sentences the same way ("The storm ..." five times in a row).
- Do not start an important sentence with a single short word like "A" or "So": the transcriber often drops it.
- No parentheses, slashes or symbols.

LENGTH
About 150 spoken words per minute: 2 minutes is about 300 words, 5 minutes about 750, 10 minutes about 1,500.

FACTS
Write only what the topic you were given supports. If it does not say something, do not add it. End NEWS and POST-EVENT
scripts with what comes next or how to stay safe, and a FORECAST with the week ahead.
