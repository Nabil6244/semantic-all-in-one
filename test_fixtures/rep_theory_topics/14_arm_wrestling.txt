Title: Why Training Tendons Is Basically Cheating at Arm Wrestling
Training your tendons can make you win arm wrestles against guys way bigger than you. The muscles that close your hand don't sit in your hand. They sit up in your forearm, and their force reaches your fingers through tendons. Stiff tendons get it there fast, and in an arm wrestle, late loses. Every match comes down to four moments. The go, the wrist, the squeeze, and the line. You've seen it happen. The ref says go, and the guy with the giant bicep just folds. Because a big bicep can't save you if your wrist is already bent back. Now, size does help. That's why pro arm wrestling has weight classes. But most big guys only ever train their biceps. They've never trained a single one of these four moments. And the last one, the line, is also how you never end up in one of those videos where someone's arm breaks. Researchers watched 74 of them. I'll show you what 8 out of 10 had in common. But first, a quick test. Sit at a table, rest your forearm on it, palm up, with your hand hanging off the edge. Hold a backpack with a few books in it, about five kilos. Now curl your wrist up toward you and hold it there. The moment your knuckles drop below the table, stop the clock. Then comment your time. At the end, I'll tell you what your time means and which hold to start with. And if you want every tendon trained, not just your arm, we wrote it all into a 12-week plan. Every hold, how heavy, how long. It's called tendon theory, and it's linked below. But everything for arm wrestling is right here, so let's start. Moment one, the go. Between normal guys, most arm wrestlers are decided in the first second. Whoever gets his force into the hand first takes the wrist, and usually the match. How fast your force arrives depends partly on your tendons. In one study of 16 trained men, the ones with stiffer tendons built force faster. Tendon stiffness alone explained up to about 30% of the difference. They measured it in the thigh, but every muscle works the same way. It pulls on a tendon, and the tendon pulls on the bone. A stretchy tendon is like slack in a rope. You pull, and for a split second nothing happens. Then it catches. In an arm wrestle, that split second is when your wrist gets taken. So the first move trains the go. The towel pull. Loop a towel around something that can't move or tip. A heavy table leg or a railing works. Sit side on, elbow bent at about 90 degrees, hand at chest height, wrist curled in. On go, pull as hard and as fast as you can, and hold it for three seconds. Then rest for three seconds. Five pulls make one set. Nothing moves, but your tendons are taking a full load fast. That's exactly what they need. Moment two, the wrist. Your hand is the very end of the lever. If your wrist bends back, your bicep and your shoulder are pushing through the weakest link, and they can't help you. Arm wrestlers fix this with what they call cupping. Curl your wrist in toward you, and keep it there. If you saw our tendon video, you already know holds trained tendons. But here's the part that decides if they work. In one study, people trained one leg with a light load, and the other leg with a heavy load. Same reps, same weeks. After 14 weeks, only the heavy leg's tendon got stiffer and thicker. The light leg got nothing beyond what everyday life already gives it. A review of 27 studies found the same pattern. What changes a tendon is how heavy the load is. So go heavy and short. Move to the cup hold. Same position as the test, but now add books until you can hold the top for only about eight seconds. Hold for three, rest for three. Five times is one set. Move three, the hammer rise. Stand with your arms straight down at your side, holding a hammer or a pan by the very end of the handle, thumb pointing forward. Now lift the head up toward you, using only your wrist, and hold it for three seconds. The closer your hand is to the end of the handle, the heavier it gets. Arm wrestlers call this rising. It's how you get over the top of his hand. Moment three, the squeeze. When a match stalls in the middle, the guy whose grip slips loses his wrist next. The tendons that close your fingers run all the way from your forearm through your wrist to your fingertips. They're some of the longest tendons in your arm, and every second of a stalled match, they're holding the load. Move four, the heavy bucket hold. Fill a bucket with water or sand until you can only hold it for 20 to 30 seconds. Stand tall, hold it at your side with one hand, and squeeze the handle as hard as you can the whole time. Shoulders down. Don't let it pull you sideways. When 30 seconds gets easy, make the bucket heavier. Never just hold it longer. And moment four, the line. The one from the start. Researchers watched 74 videos of arm wrestling matches where someone's upper arm broke. In about 8 out of 10, the injured guy's arm had lost its line. His hand had drifted behind his shoulder, out of line with his elbow. Twisting the upper body was linked to it too. Your upper arm bone isn't built to take that kind of twist. So here's the rule. Face your hand. Keep it in front of your shoulder, close to your chest, in one straight line with your elbow. Never turn your head or your body away from your arm. And if he has you beat and your hand starts getting pulled behind you, let go. Losing one match is nothing. A hospital study of these injuries found one more thing. Cold weather and skipping the warm-up made them more likely. So never arm wrestle cold. A minute of wrist circles and a few easy pulls first. And here's the good news. The line isn't just safer. It's stronger. With your hand close and in front of you, your whole body is behind it. Practice it on every towel pull. So here's the plan. Three days a week with a day off in between. Warm-up first, then the towel pull, three sets of five. The cup hold, three sets. The hammer rise, three sets on each hand. And the bucket hold, two or three holds per hand. Every hold is short and heavy. When a hold gets easy, add weight, not time. The whole session takes about 20 minutes. Under 18, keep every hold at about seven out of 10, not all out. Your grip will feel stronger within a few weeks. Your tendons take longer. That study ran 14 weeks. So think three months, not three days. Now your cup test. Under 20 seconds, start the cup hold with a lighter bag and you'll improve the fastest. 20 to 40 seconds, you're where most guys start. Run the full plan. 40 to 60, your wrist is already strong, so make the towel pull your main move. Over 60, add books until the test is hard again. That's our own rough guide, not a lab test.
