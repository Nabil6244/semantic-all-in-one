On September 5, 1977, a small spacecraft left Earth carrying something far bigger than its size suggested: a message from humanity. It was Voyager 1, launched on a trajectory designed to visit the giant planets of the outer Solar System. Its journey would eventually take it farther from the Sun than any human-made object had ever gone. First came Jupiter. In 1979, Voyager 1 swept past the largest planet in our Solar System, capturing detailed images of its enormous storms and discovering previously unknown features, including a thin ring and two new moons. Then the spacecraft continued toward Saturn. In 1980, Voyager 1 passed Saturn and made a close encounter with its giant moon Titan. That encounter changed everything. Gravity from Saturn redirected Voyager 1 onto a path that would carry it outward, away from the plane of the planets and toward the edge of the Sun’s influence. But the planetary tour was only the beginning. For more than two decades, Voyager 1 crossed an increasingly distant and unfamiliar region of space. In December 2004, after traveling about 94 times the distance between Earth and the Sun, it crossed the termination shock, where the solar wind suddenly slowed and became turbulent. Then came the heliosheath, the outer region of the Sun’s enormous bubble. On August 25, 2012, Voyager 1 finally crossed the heliopause at about 122 astronomical units from the Sun. For the first time, a human-made spacecraft had entered interstellar space. And Voyager did not find an empty void. Its instruments began measuring particles and magnetic fields from the space between stars, giving humanity its first direct measurements from beyond the Sun’s protective bubble. Voyager 1 began as a mission to explore Jupiter and Saturn. But its real destination became something much larger. The space between the stars.
