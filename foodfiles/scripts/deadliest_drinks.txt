The Deadliest Drinks Ever Sold

From a ginger tonic that left tens of thousands of people unable to walk, to a sweet raspberry syrup that killed more than a hundred people, these are the deadliest drinks ever sold. But first, we're starting with soda, because there's a good chance you drank this one as a kid.

This is brominated vegetable oil, or BVO, and for decades it was hiding in citrus sodas like Mountain Dew, Sun Drop and Squirt. And you've never tasted it, because that was kind of the point. Citrus flavor comes from oils, and oil floats, so without help the flavor would rise to the top of the bottle. So drink makers bonded the oil to bromine, which made it heavy enough to stay mixed in. Sounds harmless, right? Well, bromine is the same element used in flame retardants, and your body doesn't flush it out very well. It builds up. And doctors have written up cases of people who drank huge amounts of these sodas and ended up with bromine poisoning. According to one case report from 2003, a man who drank about eight liters of Ruby Red Squirt a day developed painful sores across his skin. And to put that in perspective, eight liters is more than twenty cans. But here's the part that gets people. The FDA had already pulled BVO off its safe list back in 1970, and then just let companies keep using it for more than fifty years. In 2012, a fifteen-year-old from Hattiesburg, Mississippi named Sarah Kavanagh started an online petition against BVO in Gatorade. Within a year Gatorade dropped it, and by 2014 Coca-Cola and PepsiCo had pulled it too. And in July 2024, the FDA finally banned it for good. So yeah, a teenager beat the FDA by twelve years. But at least BVO stayed in your fat cells, because this next drink stayed in your bones.

This is Radithor, a little bottle of water with real radium dissolved inside, sold in the 1920s as a health tonic. And back then, radium was the hot new miracle. It glowed, it was in the news, and people believed it could cure anything from tiredness to arthritis. So in 1927, a rich Pittsburgh steel man and champion golfer named Eben Byers hurt his arm, and his doctor told him to try Radithor. And Byers loved it. He said it made him feel young again, so he kept drinking it. By most estimates, he drank close to fourteen hundred bottles. So what actually happened? Well, radium acts a lot like calcium, so your body builds it right into your bones, where it keeps giving off radiation from the inside. Byers' bones started falling apart. Holes opened in his skull, and doctors had to remove most of his jaw. In 1932, Byers died at fifty-one, and he was buried in a coffin lined with lead. As a result, the government cracked down on radioactive medicine, and the man who made Radithor, William Bailey, was ordered to stop. Bailey had no medical degree, by the way. Not exactly a great look. But while Radithor's danger was hidden inside the bottle, this next one was hidden by the bottle itself.

Because this is a bottle of ginger beer in Paisley, Scotland, and it changed the law for every company on Earth. On August 26th, 1928, a woman named May Donoghue went to a cafe with a friend, who bought her a ginger beer float. And the bottle was dark brown glass, so you couldn't see inside. She drank about half of it, and when her friend poured the rest into her glass, out slid what looked like a rotting snail. Donoghue got sick with stomach pain and shock. But here's the problem. Back then, she couldn't sue the cafe, because she hadn't bought the drink herself. So she sued the man who made it, a drink maker named David Stevenson. And the case went all the way to the highest court in Britain. In 1932, the judges ruled that companies owe a duty of care to anyone who uses their products, not just the person who paid. That ruling is still the base of product safety law today. But here's the twist. Nobody ever proved there was a snail in that bottle. Stevenson died before the trial, and the case was settled with his family for two hundred pounds. So the most famous snail in legal history might not have existed. But at least that snail only made one person sick, because this next drink made dozens of kids sick, and it was sold as the healthy choice.

We're talking about Odwalla, the fresh juice brand in the glass bottles that tasted like it came straight from the orchard. And that was the whole pitch. Odwalla didn't heat its juice to kill germs, because heating changes the taste, and the company said its washing process was enough. Well, in October 1996, people across the western United States and Canada started getting very sick with a dangerous strain of E. coli. And the one thing they had in common was Odwalla apple juice. In the end, sixty-six people got sick, most of them children, and fourteen kids developed a kind of kidney failure. A sixteen-month-old girl in Colorado named Anna Gimmestad died. And investigators later reported that apples with damage and rot had gone into the juice. As a result, Odwalla recalled its apple products, started heat-treating its juice, and in 1998 pleaded guilty to federal charges and paid a one and a half million dollar fine, one of the biggest in a food poisoning case at the time. And the FDA ended up requiring warning labels on unpasteurized juice. Healthy, until it wasn't. But while Odwalla's problem was sitting inside the fruit, this next drink's problem came from the factory pipes.

This is Perrier, the fancy French sparkling water in the green bottle. In the 1980s, it was the drink of rich people who wanted to look healthy. But in early 1990, a lab in North Carolina was using Perrier as its clean water for testing, because it was supposed to be pure. And the tests kept picking up benzene, a chemical linked to cancer. So at first, Perrier blamed one mistake at one plant. Then it said the problem was only in North America. Then they found benzene in bottles in Europe too. By February 1990, Perrier was recalling a hundred and sixty million bottles from around the world. And the real cause was dirty filters at the source, where the gas was being handled. But here's the part that hurt even more than the recall. For years, the label said "naturally sparkling", but the investigation showed the bubbles were actually taken out and added back in. So Perrier had to drop the word "naturally" from its labels. Luckily, nobody was reported sick. But imagine paying extra for water and getting chemistry class. But at least Perrier knew what was in its bottles, because this next company never figured it out.

This is Coca-Cola in Belgium, in June 1999. And one morning, students at a school in the town of Bornem drank Coke at lunch and started feeling sick. Headaches, nausea, dizziness. Within days, more than a hundred people across Belgium and France said Coke made them ill, and a lot of them were kids. So Belgium did something no country had ever done to Coca-Cola. It banned the sale of every single Coke product in the country. Coca-Cola pulled millions of cans and bottles from shelves in four countries, the biggest recall in its 113-year history at the time. So what was in the cans? Well, the company found a bad batch of carbon dioxide at one plant, and a chemical used to treat wooden pallets that had gotten onto the outside of some cans. But here's the weird part. Scientists who studied it later said the amounts were far too small to make anyone that sick, and many believed most of the illness was mass panic, spreading from kid to kid. Still, the damage was done. Coke lost millions, and later that year its CEO announced he was stepping down. Nobody can say for sure what happened. But if you think a mystery Coke is as bad as it gets, we still got a ginger tonic that paralyzed a nation, and a children's medicine made with the same chemical as antifreeze. But first, this next drink was poured into almost every family's glass.

This is swill milk, and in the 1850s it was the milk most families in New York City were drinking. And here's how it was made. Next to the city's whiskey distilleries were huge, packed stables full of cows, and those cows were fed one thing, the hot leftover mash from making whiskey. The cows got so sick that some couldn't stand, and workers would milk them anyway. The milk came out thin and bluish, so dealers mixed in chalk and plaster to make it look white, and sold it as fresh country milk. And in 1858, a newspaper called Frank Leslie's Illustrated ran an exposé blaming swill milk for the deaths of as many as eight thousand children a year in New York. As a result, the public was furious, and in 1862 New York State passed a law against selling swill milk. And it would take decades more of fighting before milk was finally pasteurized. So the next time milk smells a little off, just remember it used to have plaster in it. But at least swill milk looked a little strange, because this next drink looked like medicine, and that's exactly how it got past the law.

We're talking about Jamaica Ginger, or Jake, and during Prohibition it was one of the easiest ways to get a drink in America. Because alcohol was banned, but medicine wasn't. And Jake was a ginger extract sold at pharmacies that was about seventy percent alcohol. To stop people drinking it, the government made the makers pack it with so much ginger that it tasted awful. So in 1930, two businessmen in Boston, Harry Gross and Max Reisman, looked for a cheaper way to pass the government's test. And they swapped some of the ginger for a chemical used in plastics called TOCP. It passed the test. But here's the thing nobody knew yet. TOCP attacks the nerves. In the spring of 1930, people who drank Jake started losing control of their feet and legs. They walked with a slapping, stumbling step that people called the Jake Walk, and some could never walk normally again. Estimates of how many were hurt range from tens of thousands to as many as a hundred thousand people. It was so widespread that blues singers wrote songs about the "Jake Leg". And in the end, Gross and Reisman were convicted, but most of the victims never got anything. A whole nation, damaged by one cost-cutting shortcut. But while Jake was sold to adults who knew it was booze, this next one was sold for children.

This is Elixir Sulfanilamide, and in 1937 it was supposed to be a breakthrough. Sulfanilamide was one of the first real antibiotics, and it worked. But it came as a pill, and kids hate pills. So a company in Bristol, Tennessee called S. E. Massengill wanted a liquid. Their chief chemist, Harold Watkins, found that the drug dissolved perfectly in a sweet clear liquid called diethylene glycol. Then he added raspberry flavor, and the company shipped it across the country. And nobody tested it on animals first, because back then, the law didn't say they had to. Well, diethylene glycol is a close cousin of antifreeze, and it destroys the kidneys. Within weeks, people who took the elixir were dying in agony, and many of them were children with sore throats. The FDA sent almost every inspector it had to track down every bottle, and they recovered most of them. But by the end, more than a hundred people were dead. And here's the part that made people furious. The company was only fined about twenty-six thousand dollars, and not for poisoning anyone, but because the word "elixir" meant it had to contain alcohol, and it didn't. The chemist, Harold Watkins, took his own life. As a result, in 1938, Congress passed the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, the law that still makes companies prove a drug is safe before they sell it. One bottle rewrote the rules. But while that elixir was a new chemical, this last drink is one of the oldest in the world.

Because this is beer, and in 1900, the people of Manchester, England were drinking the deadliest batch in history. And at first, nobody knew why. People started showing up with burning hands and feet, numb legs and darkened skin, and doctors blamed it on drinking too much. Thousands of people across the north of England got sick. Well, a doctor named Ernest Reynolds noticed that the patients had something in common, and it wasn't how much they drank, it was which beer they drank. And it turned out the brewers were using a cheap sugar to speed up brewing. That sugar was made with sulfuric acid, and the acid came from a supplier whose raw material was loaded with arsenic. So without knowing it, the brewers were pouring poison into thousands of pints. In the end, more than six thousand people were poisoned and at least seventy died. As a result, the government set up a royal commission, led by the famous scientist Lord Kelvin, which recommended some of the first limits on arsenic in food and drink. But here's the twist. When the commission tested beer from other towns, it found arsenic there too, and not from the sugar at all. It was coming from the malt, which was being dried over coal fires, and the coal smoke carried arsenic right into the grain. So the poison wasn't one bad supplier, it was the way beer was being made. So the next time someone says they need a beer to calm their nerves, remember these ones did the opposite.

So the next time you grab a drink from the fridge, maybe flip it around and read what's actually inside.
