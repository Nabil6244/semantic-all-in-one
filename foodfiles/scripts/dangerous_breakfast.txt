The Most Dangerous Breakfast Foods Ever Sold

From a wave of syrup that flattened a city block, to a melon that killed thirty-three people, these are the most dangerous breakfast foods ever sold. But first, we're starting with a cereal you definitely had as a kid, because in 2022 it turned into one of the strangest mysteries in food.

This is Lucky Charms, the oat cereal with the little marshmallow hearts, stars and rainbows, and a leprechaun on the box. It's been around since 1964, and it's about as harmless as breakfast gets, right? Well, in the spring of 2022, people started posting online that a bowl of Lucky Charms had made them sick. Stomach cramps, vomiting, diarrhea, and some of them said it happened every single time they ate it. And it wasn't just a few people. A food safety website called I Was Poisoned collected more than three thousand reports in a few weeks, way more than it had ever gotten about any one product. Some people even posted photos of the cereal they'd eaten, and nobody could spot anything wrong with it. So the FDA opened an investigation, and it got more than a hundred complaints of its own. But here's the weird part. General Mills tested its cereal and said it found nothing wrong, no bacteria, no bad ingredient, nothing. The FDA looked into it too, and nobody ever found a cause. So to this day, there's no official explanation for why thousands of people said the same cereal made them sick. Some experts think a lot of it was the power of suggestion, people reading the posts and blaming their bowl for an ordinary stomach bug. Others still think something in the cereal changed. Magically delicious, magically mysterious. But at least Lucky Charms never got recalled, because this next cereal did, and it had the opposite problem: everybody knew exactly what was in it.

This is Cheerios, the little oat circles that have been in American kitchens since 1941. And in the summer of 2015, General Mills made a big announcement. Cheerios was going gluten-free, which was huge news for people with celiac disease, who get sick from even tiny amounts of wheat. And the company spent a fortune telling everyone about it. Well, a few months later, in October 2015, General Mills recalled about one point eight million boxes of gluten-free Cheerios and Honey Nut Cheerios. So what happened? At a plant in Lodi, California, wheat flour had been accidentally delivered into the system that was supposed to carry only gluten-free oat flour. So the boxes said gluten-free, and the cereal inside had wheat in it. And more than a hundred people with celiac disease reported getting sick. For them, it's not a little tummy ache, it can mean days of pain and damage to the gut. The company apologized, and it changed how it handles flour at the plant. So yeah, the one cereal you'd trust to be safe was the one that wasn't. But at least that was one mistake at one plant, because this next breakfast was recalled more than half a billion times.

We're talking about eggs, and in 2010 America had the biggest egg recall in its history. It started in the summer of 2010, when health officials noticed a huge jump in a type of salmonella, and the cases all traced back to eggs. And those eggs came from two big farms in Iowa, Wright County Egg and Hillandale Farms. And both farms were linked to the same man, a businessman named Austin DeCoster, who had been in trouble with regulators for decades. So in August 2010, the two companies recalled around five hundred and fifty million eggs, sold under dozens of different brand names in stores across the country. To put that in perspective, that's more than one egg for every person in the United States and Canada combined. In the end, nearly two thousand people were reported sick. And when inspectors walked into the Iowa barns, they found rodents, flies and maggots, and piles of manure as tall as eight feet. But here's the part that almost never happens. In 2015, the owner, Austin DeCoster, and his son Peter were each sentenced to three months in jail. And that's rare, because food company bosses almost never go behind bars for an outbreak. So yeah, a bad egg went to jail. But while those eggs made people sick by accident, this next breakfast food can show up on a drug test.

This is a poppy seed muffin, and it might be the only breakfast that can get you fired. Because poppy seeds come from the opium poppy, the same plant used to make morphine and heroin. And the seeds themselves don't contain much, but the outside of the seed can pick up traces of morphine from the plant. So if you eat a poppy seed bagel or muffin right before a drug test, there's a real chance it comes back positive for opiates. Sounds fake, right? Well, it happened so often that in 1998, the federal government raised its drug test limit for morphine so that bagels would stop failing people. But here's where it gets dangerous. Some people figured out you could soak unwashed poppy seeds in water and drink it as a tea. And in 2016, a twenty-four-year-old named Stephen Hacala from Arkansas drank poppy seed tea and died from a morphine overdose. His parents started pushing to get unwashed poppy seeds pulled from stores, and they found the levels in some bags were high enough to kill. Not exactly what you expect from a muffin. But at least poppy seeds only hurt the person eating them, because this next breakfast food was at the center of a heist.

Because this is maple syrup, and in Canada it's basically liquid gold. Quebec makes most of the world's maple syrup, and it keeps a giant emergency stockpile, kind of like an oil reserve, but for pancakes. And in 2012, during a routine check of a warehouse in a small town called Saint-Louis-de-Blandford, an inspector climbed onto a barrel and almost fell over. Because it was supposed to be full of syrup, and it was nearly empty. So what happened? Well, over about a year, a group of men had been sneaking in, draining the syrup into their own barrels, and refilling the originals with water so nobody would notice. Then they sold the stolen syrup to buyers in other provinces and across the border in the United States. In the end, around three thousand tons of syrup went missing, worth about eighteen million dollars. It's now known as the Great Canadian Maple Syrup Heist. And in 2017, the ringleader, Richard Vallières, was sentenced to eight years in prison and ordered to pay a fine of over nine million dollars. Nobody got hurt, but some pancakes went very dry. But while that syrup disappeared quietly, this next one came out all at once, and it was deadly.

This is molasses, the thick dark syrup people use in baking, and on January 15th, 1919, it flooded the streets of Boston. Because in the North End of the city stood a giant steel tank, about fifty feet tall, holding more than two million gallons of molasses. And people living nearby said the tank leaked so badly that kids would collect the drips. The company had even painted it brown to hide the leaks. So around lunchtime that day, the tank burst. And a wave of molasses, by some accounts up to twenty-five feet high, swept through the neighborhood at around thirty-five miles an hour. It knocked buildings off their foundations, bent the steel of an elevated railway, and trapped people and horses in thick, freezing syrup. And because it was January, the molasses got colder and thicker by the minute, which made it even harder for rescuers to pull people out. Sailors from a navy ship nearby ran in to help, and the cleanup took weeks. In the end, twenty-one people died and about a hundred and fifty were hurt. And as a result, the families sued the company behind the tank, and after years in court, it had to pay out more than six hundred thousand dollars. But here's the creepy part. People in Boston said that for decades afterward, on a hot summer day, you could still smell molasses in the North End. But if you think a syrup flood is as bad as it gets, we still got a baby formula that emptied store shelves across the country, and a fruit that caused one of the deadliest outbreaks in American history. But first, this next one sounds dangerous on purpose.

This is Death Wish Coffee, a brand from upstate New York that sells itself as the world's strongest coffee, with a skull and crossbones right on the bag. A man named Mike Brown started it in 2012 out of a small coffee shop, and it blew up so fast that in 2016 it won a free ad during the Super Bowl. And for years, the danger was just good marketing. But in 2019, the company was selling a canned nitro cold brew, and the FDA flagged a problem. Cold brew coffee isn't very acidic, and the cans were sealed with nitrogen and sold on regular shelves, not in a fridge. And those are almost exactly the conditions where a bacteria called botulism can grow, which makes a toxin that can paralyze you. And to put that in perspective, botulism is so dangerous that a tiny amount of the toxin can stop you from breathing. So in September 2019, Death Wish recalled its eleven-ounce nitro cold brew cans, stopped selling them, and went back to the drawing board. Luckily, nobody was reported sick. But imagine the headline. A coffee called Death Wish, pulled for a deadly toxin. You honestly can't make this stuff up. And at least that recall came before anyone got hurt, because this next one is probably in your cupboard right now.

This is Jif peanut butter, and if you're from the US, you probably know the slogan: choosy moms choose Jif. Well, in May 2022, choosy moms were choosing to throw it in the trash. Because the CDC traced a salmonella outbreak to Jif made at a single factory in Lexington, Kentucky. In the end, twenty-one people in seventeen states got sick, and four of them ended up in the hospital. But here's the twist. Jif isn't only sold in jars. It goes into crackers, snack packs, candy, ice cream and baked goods made by other companies. So when Jif recalled its peanut butter, dozens of other products got recalled along with it, from fudge to apple dipping cups. And families had to go through their cupboards checking the code on every lid, because the recalled jars were the ones whose codes ended in four two five, the number of that one factory. And according to the FDA, the same strain of salmonella had been found at that factory years before. Smucker's had to shut the Lexington plant down while it cleaned and rebuilt parts of it, and the empty shelves lasted for weeks. So the next time a kid asks for peanut butter on toast, you might want to check the label first. But while the Jif recall was mostly about adults, this next one was about the tiniest customers there are.

We're talking about baby formula. And in February 2022, the company Abbott recalled powdered formulas like Similac, Alimentum and EleCare, all made at one plant in Sturgis, Michigan. Because four babies who drank formula from that plant had gotten sick with a rare bacteria called Cronobacter, and two of them died. Abbott said there was no proof its formula caused the illnesses. But inspectors found Cronobacter inside the plant, along with standing water and cracks in the equipment. And here's the part that made people furious. Months earlier, in October 2021, a former worker had sent the FDA a long report warning about problems at that exact plant. So the plant shut down, and because that one plant made such a big share of America's formula, the shelves started emptying out. By May 2022, around forty percent of baby formula was out of stock nationwide. Parents drove from store to store, and the government had to fly in formula from Europe in something called Operation Fly Formula. And just weeks after the plant finally reopened, a storm flooded it and shut it down all over again. All from one factory in Michigan. But while that formula was meant for babies, this last breakfast food was mostly deadly for grandparents.

Because this is cantaloupe, the orange melon that shows up in every hotel breakfast and fruit cup. And in the summer of 2011, a farm in Colorado called Jensen Farms shipped out melons that looked perfectly fine. But they were carrying listeria, a germ that can live in cold fridges and hits older people the hardest. So that fall, people across the country started getting sick, and some got a deadly brain infection. And listeria is sneaky, because it can take weeks after you eat something to make you sick, so by the time doctors figured out it was the melons, many of them had already been eaten. In the end, a hundred and forty-seven people in twenty-eight states got sick, and thirty-three of them died, most of them over sixty. It was the deadliest food outbreak in the United States in nearly a hundred years. So what went wrong? Well, investigators found that the farm had bought new washing equipment that was meant for potatoes, and it didn't use the germ-killing wash that usually cleans the fruit. And the dirty water and the packing shed gave listeria a place to grow. As a result, in 2013, the farm's owners, brothers Eric and Ryan Jensen, pleaded guilty to federal charges. They got five years of probation and six months of home detention. A breakfast melon, and thirty-three people gone.

So the next time you pour a bowl or crack an egg in the morning, maybe take a second look at what's actually in it. And the next time you pack a lunch for work or school, be careful there too, because these are the deadliest lunch foods ever sold, and one so dangerous it sent hundreds of kids to the hospital in a single day.
